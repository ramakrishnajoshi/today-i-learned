# Useless Skills That Suddenly Become Useful

**Source:** [Reddit — r/AskReddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskReddit/s/nF1Oc2DV9t)
**Question:** *What is a talent or skill you have that is completely useless 99% of the time, but made you look like a god the one time you needed it?*

## ⭐ Memorable

> **A skill doesn't have to be useful every day to be worth having.**

Some skills sit dormant for years and then become extraordinarily valuable in one specific moment.

The interesting part isn't necessarily the skill itself. It's the combination of **curiosity + learning + remembering + being prepared**.

---

## The big picture

The thread is a collection of stories about people whose seemingly random or impractical skills suddenly became useful.

Examples included:

- Juggling calming a screaming child.
- Making paper fortune tellers entertaining children.
- Catching birds that get into classrooms.
- Handling venomous snakes.
- Repairing tractors and appliances.
- Using obscure mechanical/electrical knowledge to improvise solutions.
- Memorizing countries and using that knowledge in a timed challenge.
- Fishing when people were genuinely out of food.
- Performing CPR when someone suffered cardiac arrest.
- Knowing how to repair an oven just before Thanksgiving.
- Using knowledge of infrared LEDs to improvise a Wii sensor.

The common theme is:

**You rarely know in advance which knowledge will eventually matter.**

---

## 1. Random knowledge can have enormous situational value

A skill can be almost completely irrelevant to everyday life and still become extremely valuable in a particular situation.

One person knew how to repair small engines and tractors. Another knew how to catch birds. Another knew how to make balloon animals. Another had memorized a song containing the names of countries.

Most of the time these skills had little practical value.

Then the right situation appeared.

**Takeaway:** Don't judge every skill purely by its everyday utility.

---

## 2. CPR is the clearest example of a "rare but extremely valuable" skill

One commenter described a friend collapsing after cardiac arrest.

They immediately started chest compressions while others were panicking and called for emergency help. The friend survived, and medical professionals said the immediate CPR was a major reason.

The commenter ended with an important lesson:

> **EVERYONE should have CPR training**

CPR is a skill you may never use.

That's exactly what makes it interesting:

**Low frequency + extremely high consequence.**

You don't learn CPR because you're likely to use it tomorrow.

You learn it because if the situation ever occurs, being prepared can matter enormously.

---

## 3. Knowledge can become valuable through improvisation

Several stories weren't simply about knowing a fact.

They involved combining knowledge with whatever happened to be available.

For example, someone remembered that a Wii sensor worked using infrared LEDs and improvised a replacement using two candles when the original sensor bar was forgotten.

The important skill wasn't merely remembering the technical fact.

It was:

**Understand how something works → recognize the relevant principle → improvise with available resources.**

**Takeaway:** Understanding mechanisms is often more useful than memorizing procedures.

---

## 4. Old skills don't necessarily disappear

One person learned lock picking as a child and didn't use it for years.

When a colleague accidentally locked themselves out of an office, the old skill suddenly became useful.

Another person learned appliance repair decades earlier and later diagnosed and repaired a broken oven shortly before Thanksgiving.

The pattern is interesting:

**Unused doesn't necessarily mean forgotten.**

Skills that are deeply learned can remain available for surprisingly long periods.

---

## 5. Hobbies can create unexpected capabilities

Many of the skills in the thread came from hobbies or things people learned simply because they were interested.

Examples include:

- Martial arts.
- Fishing.
- Foraging.
- Juggling.
- Crochet.
- Balloon animals.
- Mechanics.
- Bird handling.
- Memorization.
- Musical skills.

None of these people necessarily learned the skill because they predicted it would be useful professionally.

They learned because:

**"This is interesting."**

And sometimes that turned out to be enough.

---

## 6. Being prepared can make you look like a genius

A recurring pattern in the stories is that the person didn't necessarily do anything extraordinary *in that moment*.

They simply happened to already know how to do something.

To everyone else, it looked like magic.

That's an interesting distinction:

**Preparation often looks like talent when observed at the moment it becomes useful.**

---

## 7. The value of a skill depends on context

A skill isn't inherently "useful" or "useless."

Its value depends on:

**Skill × Situation**

Knowing how to repair an oven isn't particularly useful at most family gatherings.

Until the oven breaks the day before Thanksgiving.

Knowing how to catch birds isn't particularly useful to most teachers.

Until a bird gets loose inside the classroom.

Knowing CPR isn't something most people need every day.

Until someone collapses in front of you.

**Takeaway:** Utility is contextual.

---

## 8. Curiosity has option value

A broader lesson from the thread is that learning something simply because you're curious creates future possibilities you can't predict.

You don't know:

- Who you'll meet.
- What situations you'll encounter.
- What problems you'll face.
- Which hobby will become useful.
- Which piece of knowledge will connect with something else.

This is one reason curiosity can be valuable even when there is no immediate practical payoff.

---

## What I want to remember

Not everything I learn needs to have an obvious immediate ROI.

Some knowledge is worth acquiring because:

- It's interesting.
- It expands how I understand the world.
- It gives me another way to solve problems.
- It may become unexpectedly useful later.
- It makes me more adaptable.

**Learn things because you're curious. You don't always get to choose when the knowledge becomes useful.**

---

## A useful distinction

There are two kinds of "useless" skills:

### Low-value trivia

Information that doesn't teach you anything useful and is quickly forgotten.

### Latent capabilities

Skills that aren't frequently needed but could become extremely valuable in the right situation.

The second category is worth cultivating.

Examples:

- First aid / CPR.
- Basic mechanical knowledge.
- Basic electrical knowledge.
- Cooking.
- Communication.
- Navigation.
- First-principles thinking.
- Repair skills.
- Practical problem solving.
- Knowing how things work.

The goal isn't to collect random skills.

It's to become the kind of person who can say:

**"I don't know if this will ever be useful, but I'm glad I know how to do it."**
