# What looked unimportant in your 30s but turned out to matter enormously?

**Source:** [Reddit — r/AskReddit](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskReddit/s/dlXC2leOme)  
**Question:** *What looked unimportant in your 30s but turned out to matter enormously?*

## The big picture

The thread is a collection of things people didn't take seriously enough in their 30s, only to realize later that these things had much bigger long-term consequences than expected.

The strongest recurring themes are:

**Health → Money → Relationships → Time → Habits**

The common pattern is **delayed consequences**. Many things don't feel urgent when you're young, but their effects accumulate over years and become much harder to reverse later.

---

## 1. Health compounds too

Exercise, sleep, diet, posture, mobility, dental care and general physical maintenance came up repeatedly.

The mistake is assuming that your body will continue to tolerate poor habits simply because it has done so until now.

### Things worth taking seriously

- Exercise regularly.
- Build strength.
- Maintain flexibility and mobility.
- Take sleep seriously.
- Eat reasonably well.
- Look after your teeth.
- Pay attention to posture.
- Don't ignore persistent or unusual pain.
- Keep up with preventive healthcare.

A particularly useful perspective from the thread is that fitness isn't only about appearance.

Being physically fit later means being able to:

- Climb stairs without getting exhausted.
- Carry heavy bags.
- Stand for long periods.
- Maintain balance.
- Avoid losing independence as you age.

One commenter described how things that were difficult the previous year became easy after consistently exercising.

**Takeaway:** Fitness is an investment in future independence.

---

## 2. Sleep

One of the simplest and most repeated answers was:

**Sleep.**

In your 20s and 30s, poor sleep can feel like something you can compensate for with caffeine and willpower.

The consequences can accumulate quietly through:

- Mood
- Weight
- Patience
- Memory
- Relationships
- Work performance
- Overall quality of life

The thread's memorable idea:

> Sleep debt collects interest.

**Takeaway:** Don't treat sleep as time stolen from productivity. It is part of the productivity system.

---

## 3. Flossing and dental care

Dental health appeared repeatedly.

It's easy to dismiss flossing and routine dental care because the consequences aren't immediate.

Then years of neglect can turn into:

- Root canals
- Dental procedures
- Significant expenses
- Chronic dental problems

The basic lesson:

**Take care of your teeth while they are still healthy.**

---

## 4. Start investing early

This was one of the biggest themes in the thread.

People repeatedly mentioned:

- Retirement savings
- Investing
- Compound growth
- Starting early
- Automating contributions
- Increasing contributions when income rises

The key insight is that **time is an enormous part of the equation**.

Starting earlier can matter more than making much larger contributions later.

One commenter summarized the idea:

> You can catch up on money later, but you can’t buy back decades of compounding.

### Practical lessons

- Start as early as reasonably possible.
- Automate contributions.
- Don't constantly stop and start based on market predictions.
- Increase contributions as income increases.
- Learn enough about investing and taxes to make informed decisions.

---

## 5. Don't underestimate small savings

Another interesting point was that small, regular contributions can eventually become meaningful.

A small amount that you barely notice coming out of each paycheck can eventually become:

- An emergency fund.
- A financial cushion.
- Retirement savings.
- Money for opportunities.
- A source of freedom when something unexpected happens.

The important part is **consistency**.

---

## 6. Financial literacy

The thread also highlighted that earning money isn't enough.

Understanding things such as:

- Taxes
- Investments
- Retirement accounts
- Debt
- Inflation
- Compound growth
- Saving

can have a huge effect on your long-term financial position.

**Takeaway:** Financial literacy is itself a valuable life skill.

---

## 7. Automate good financial decisions

One practical suggestion was to automate retirement contributions and gradually increase them whenever you receive a raise or promotion.

The idea is simple:

**Make saving happen before you have the opportunity to spend the money.**

Instead of trying to save whatever is left at the end of the month:

> Save first → live on what's left.

---

## 8. Friendships require effort after your 30s

This was one of the strongest non-financial themes.

In your 20s, friendships often happen automatically.

You see people because:

- You study together.
- You work together.
- You live nearby.
- You go to the same places.
- Everyone has relatively flexible schedules.

In your 30s, this changes.

Careers, marriage, children, relocation and responsibilities make spontaneous interaction much less common.

You have to **deliberately maintain friendships**.

Otherwise, you can wake up years later and realize that people you considered close friends have gradually become nothing more than social-media connections.

**Takeaway:** Good friendships don't necessarily survive automatically. Maintain them deliberately.

---

## 9. Spend time with your parents while they are healthy

This was arguably the most important emotional theme in the thread.

When you're in your 30s, your parents may still be:

- Healthy
- Independent
- Working or recently retired
- Able to travel
- Able to have long conversations
- Relatively active

It's easy to think:

> "I'll spend more time with them later."

The problem is that their health and independence can change surprisingly quickly.

One commenter pointed out that their parents may be healthy in their 60s but lose autonomy rapidly in their 70s.

By the time you have more free time, they may no longer be able to travel or interact with you in the same way.

### The deeper point

There can be a limited period when:

- Your parents are alive and healthy.
- Your siblings are around.
- Your spouse is around.
- Your children are young.
- Everyone is simultaneously able to spend meaningful time together.

That window is precious.

**Takeaway:** Don't postpone family time until life becomes less busy.

---

## 10. Your social circle affects who you become

One commenter described realizing that the people around you can either:

- Raise your standards, or
- Slowly lower them.

The people you spend time with influence:

- Your habits.
- Your ambitions.
- Your standards.
- Your worldview.
- What you consider normal.

**Takeaway:** Be selective about who gets significant access to your time and attention.

---

## 11. Build sustainable exercise habits

The thread wasn't simply saying:

> "Exercise harder."

A more useful lesson was:

**Find exercise you can continue doing for decades.**

Trying different activities until you find something you genuinely enjoy can make fitness sustainable.

Consistency matters more than periodically going extremely hard and then quitting.

One commenter specifically pointed out that beginners shouldn't push themselves so hard that they hate exercise or get injured.

**Takeaway:** The best exercise is the exercise you can keep doing.

---

## 12. Strength, flexibility and mobility

Strength training gets a lot of attention, but people also emphasized:

- Core strength
- Flexibility
- Stretching
- Mobility
- Posture
- Balance

In your 30s, you may feel fine because you're still benefiting from "residual youth."

Later, weaknesses that seemed irrelevant can suddenly become painful or limiting.

**Takeaway:** Don't wait until your body starts hurting to start maintaining it.

---

## 13. Don't dismiss persistent pain

One commenter described ignoring unexplained back and shoulder pain because they assumed it was simply part of getting older.

It eventually turned out to be a serious spinal problem.

The broader lesson isn't that every ache indicates a serious disease.

It's:

**Don't automatically normalize persistent or unusual symptoms as "just getting older."**

If something doesn't feel right or keeps getting worse, get it evaluated.

---

## 14. Diet and everyday habits

People repeatedly mentioned that they could eat almost anything in their 20s without obvious consequences.

That can change in your 30s.

Building good everyday habits becomes increasingly important:

- Cooking at home.
- Eating balanced meals.
- Maintaining a reasonable weight.
- Exercising.
- Limiting unhealthy habits.
- Creating routines that are sustainable.

The important point is that these habits don't need to be extreme.

They need to be **consistent**.

---

## 15. Don't postpone everything until "later"

This is probably the overarching theme of the entire thread.

Many people described thinking:

> "I'll worry about that later."

But eventually:

**Later arrives.**

And by then:

- Your body may have changed.
- Your parents may have aged.
- Friendships may have faded.
- Missed investment years can't be recovered.
- Bad habits may be harder to break.
- Some opportunities may no longer exist.

---

# The distilled lessons

### Health

- Sleep properly.
- Exercise consistently.
- Build strength.
- Maintain mobility and flexibility.
- Eat reasonably well.
- Take care of your teeth.
- Don't ignore persistent problems.
- Maintain preventive healthcare.

### Money

- Start investing early.
- Let compounding work for you.
- Automate savings.
- Increase savings when income increases.
- Learn about taxes and investments.
- Don't underestimate small recurring contributions.
- Don't assume you can simply "catch up later."

### Relationships

- Maintain friendships deliberately.
- Spend time with your parents while they're healthy.
- Choose your social circle carefully.
- Invest in important relationships.
- Don't assume you'll always have another opportunity.

### Habits

- Build boring habits that you can maintain for decades.
- Don't rely on motivation.
- Don't wait for a crisis before changing.
- Small actions repeated for years matter enormously.

---

## What I want to remember

A lot of the most important things in life **compound silently**.

Health compounds.

Money compounds.

Skills compound.

Relationships compound.

Habits compound.

The absence of an immediate consequence doesn't mean something isn't important.

### Don't confuse "not urgent" with "not important."

The things that matter most later are often the things that feel easiest to postpone today.

**Start early. Be consistent. Take care of your health, money and relationships before they become urgent.**
