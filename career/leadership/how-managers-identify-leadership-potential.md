# How Managers Identify Leadership Potential in a New Hire

**Source:** [Reddit — r/askmanagers](https://www.reddit.com/r/askmanagers/s/y4h7YyPia0)
**Question:** *Managers, how’d you know a new hire has potential for leadership?*

## The big picture

Leadership potential isn't simply about being the smartest person, the hardest worker, or someone who asks for more responsibility.

Across the discussion, the strongest signals were:

**Initiative → Broader thinking → Problem solving → Influence → Trust → Consistency**

A potential leader gradually starts operating beyond their formal role. They understand the broader business, identify and solve problems, improve things for other people, influence without formal authority, and become someone others naturally trust.

The most important point is that these behaviors need to be **consistent over time**.

---

## 1. Think beyond your own role

Potential leaders don't only want to understand what they have to do. They want to understand:

- Why are we doing this?
- How does this fit into the larger business?
- How does my work affect other teams?
- Why does this process exist?
- Where are the opportunities to improve?

The ability to understand how different teams and functions fit together becomes increasingly important as someone moves toward leadership.

**Takeaway:** Don't just understand your job. Understand the business around your job.

---

## 2. Take initiative

One of the strongest ideas in the thread was the **Proactive Pyramid**:

1. There is a problem.
2. There is a problem, and I've identified some causes.
3. Here is the problem, possible causes, and possible solutions.
4. Here is the problem, what I think caused it, possible solutions, and what I recommend.
5. I identified the problem, figured out the cause, researched the solution, fixed it, and kept you informed.

The progression is:

**Problem reporting → Problem ownership → Problem resolution**

For a new employee, the problems don't need to be major company problems. Even something as simple as being unable to complete a task can demonstrate the same mentality:

- What is preventing me?
- Is it knowledge?
- Tools?
- Access?
- Time?
- Who can help?
- What can I try myself first?

**Takeaway:** Don't just bring problems upward. Bring analysis, options and recommendations.

---

## 3. Problem solving isn't the same as leadership

A person can be excellent at identifying problems, finding causes and implementing solutions while still being a poor leader.

Leadership also requires:

- Influencing people.
- Building trust.
- Empathy.
- Communication.
- Authenticity.
- People skills.

Technical competence is important, but it is only one part of leadership.

**Takeaway:** Being able to solve every problem yourself doesn't automatically make you someone people want to follow.

---

## 4. Build trust

One perspective in the discussion connected leadership to three qualities:

- **Competence**
- **Empathy**
- **Authenticity**

People need to believe that you know what you're doing, genuinely care about the people involved, and are being genuine with them.

Solutions and execution demonstrate competence, but competence alone isn't enough.

**Takeaway:** Leadership is built on trust, not just expertise.

---

## 5. Make your manager's stress go down

One of the strongest ideas in the thread was this:

When something unexpected happens and you explain it to your manager, does their stress level:

**Go up?**

or

**Go down?**

A high-potential employee increasingly becomes someone whose manager can trust them with difficult situations.

Instead of:

> We have a problem.

They bring:

- What happened.
- What they know.
- What they've already done.
- What they think caused it.
- Possible solutions.
- Their recommendation.
- What they need from the manager.

**Takeaway:** Become the person who reduces uncertainty and stress rather than creating more of it.

---

## 6. People naturally come to you

Informal leadership often appears before formal authority.

People may start coming to someone for:

- Advice.
- Guidance.
- Answers.
- Problem solving.
- Decisions.
- Help.

This happens because people trust their judgment.

**Takeaway:** If people naturally seek your guidance, you're already demonstrating some leadership behavior.

---

## 7. Improve the system, not just yourself

A high performer can become extremely efficient at completing their own work.

A potential leader starts improving things for everyone:

- Workflows.
- Processes.
- Quality.
- Documentation.
- Recurring problems.
- Team productivity.

The progression is:

**Personal productivity → Team productivity → Organizational leverage**

**Takeaway:** Leadership is about multiplying the effectiveness of people and systems around you.

---

## 8. Leadership without authority

A particularly useful test is:

**How do you behave when you don't have authority?**

Do you:

- Take ownership?
- Help others without being asked?
- Communicate clearly?
- Stay calm when things go wrong?
- Influence people?
- Take responsibility?

Good leadership isn't necessarily being the loudest or most confident person in the room.

---

## 9. Judgment matters

Leadership isn't about pushing your opinion every time.

You need to know:

- When to push.
- When to compromise.
- Which battles matter.
- When alignment is more important than being right.
- When a disagreement has significant long-term consequences.

**Takeaway:** Leadership requires judgment, not just confidence.

---

## 10. Be receptive to feedback

Potential leaders need to work with many different personalities.

A useful signal is how someone responds when given feedback.

Do they become defensive?

Or do they:

- Listen.
- Ask questions.
- Try to understand.
- Adjust their behavior.
- Discuss disagreements constructively.

**Takeaway:** Being coachable is part of leadership potential.

---

## 11. Character matters

Several comments pointed toward qualities such as:

- Empathy.
- Honesty.
- Sharing credit.
- Integrity.
- Humility.

Skills can be developed.

Character is much harder to change.

**Takeaway:** Don't optimize only for competence. Become someone people trust and respect.

---

## 12. Understand what senior leadership actually cares about

One of the most practical comments focused on communication with senior leaders.

Don't confuse:

**"I did a lot of work."**

with:

**"I created useful business impact."**

If leadership cares about things such as revenue, customer retention, delivery, cost or risk, understand those priorities and connect your work to them.

A useful communication pattern is:

1. What is happening?
2. Why is it happening?
3. What is the impact?
4. What do you recommend?
5. What should happen next?
6. What do you need from the senior leader?

Senior leaders have limited time.

**Takeaway:** Don't merely demonstrate subject-matter expertise. Demonstrate that you understand what the organization actually cares about.

---

## 13. Tell your manager about your ambitions

Doing excellent work isn't always enough.

If you want to grow into leadership:

- Communicate your career goals.
- Ask what skills you need to develop.
- Ask for opportunities to demonstrate leadership.
- Ask for feedback.
- Make your ambitions visible.

Don't assume that putting your head down and doing good work will automatically result in promotion.

**Takeaway:** Performance creates credibility; communicating your ambitions creates opportunity.

---

## 14. Your manager and organization matter

Career advancement isn't entirely about individual performance.

Consider:

- Is there room for growth?
- Is the organization expanding?
- Does your manager develop people?
- Will your manager advocate for you?
- Is your manager ambitious?
- Are there opportunities to demonstrate leadership?

A strong employee can still stagnate in an organization with no room for advancement.

One memorable idea from the discussion:

**Don't work for someone less ambitious than you are.**

---

## 15. Don't judge too early

Several managers pointed out that it can be difficult to evaluate a new hire during the honeymoon phase.

Someone may initially appear highly motivated simply because the job is new.

Leadership potential becomes much clearer after observing someone over time.

**Consistency matters.**

---

# Leadership self-assessment

### Ownership
- Do I identify problems before someone tells me about them?
- Do I investigate causes?
- Do I propose solutions?
- Do I take action?

### Business thinking
- Do I understand why my work matters?
- Do I understand how my team fits into the larger organization?
- Do I think about outcomes rather than just tasks?

### People
- Do people naturally come to me for help?
- Can I influence people without authority?
- Do I listen properly?
- Can I receive feedback without becoming defensive?
- Do I share credit?

### Judgment
- Do I know when to push?
- Do I know when to compromise?
- Can I distinguish important problems from unimportant ones?

### Communication
- Can I explain complicated things simply?
- Can I get to the important point quickly?
- Do I bring recommendations rather than simply reporting problems?
- Can I communicate in terms of business outcomes?

### Trust
- Can my manager give me a difficult problem and stop worrying about it?
- Do people trust my judgment?
- Do I remain calm when things go wrong?

---

## What I want to remember

**Don't wait for the leadership title to start behaving like a leader.**

Start by:

1. Taking ownership.
2. Thinking beyond your immediate role.
3. Solving problems proactively.
4. Making your manager's life easier.
5. Helping the people around you.
6. Building trust.
7. Learning to communicate in terms of business outcomes.
8. Demonstrating these behaviors consistently.

### The key distinction

A high performer says:

**"Give me a task and I'll do it extremely well."**

A potential leader gradually becomes someone who says:

**"I see a problem. I understand why it is happening. I have an idea for how we should fix it. I can get the right people aligned, execute it, and keep you informed."**

The strongest signal may ultimately be:

**People start relying on you before you have the authority to make them rely on you.**
