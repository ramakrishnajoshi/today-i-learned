# What to Do With Free Time After AI Makes You Faster

**Source:** [Reddit — r/developersIndia](https://www.reddit.com/r/developersIndia/s/trzBVPYRda)
**Question:** *Software developer with too much free time at work — what should I do?*

## ⭐ Memorable

> **What would make me better at my current job in 6 months?**

Instead of constantly asking:

> What trending technology should I learn next?

Ask:

> What would make me substantially better at the work I already do?

That could mean understanding unfamiliar parts of the codebase, deployment and monitoring, architecture, debugging, documentation, or helping other engineers.

---

## The big picture

The original poster finishes assigned work quickly using AI tools and ends up with significant free time at work. They tried filling that time with new technologies but found constantly chasing trends exhausting. :contentReference[oaicite:1]{index=1}

The responses broadly fall into several camps:

1. **Go deeper into your existing system and domain.**
2. **Use the extra capacity to build things.**
3. **Learn fundamentals rather than endlessly chasing trends.**
4. **Explore AI itself and understand what AI-generated code is doing.**
5. **Work on problems nobody else wants to touch.**
6. **Prepare for the next stage of your career.**
7. **Don't feel obligated to manufacture work just because you're efficient.**

---

## 1. Don't automatically fill every free hour with more work

One of the more thoughtful responses challenged the assumption that free time must always be converted into additional productivity.

Finishing your assigned work efficiently doesn't necessarily mean you need to manufacture additional work every day.

Some downtime is legitimate.

The important distinction is between:

**Healthy downtime**

and

**Having no meaningful growth or contribution left in your current role.**

---

## 2. Go deeper instead of constantly going wider

A particularly useful suggestion was to stop chasing every new technology.

Instead, go deeper into the system you already work on:

- Read unfamiliar parts of the codebase.
- Understand deployment.
- Understand monitoring.
- Learn the architecture.
- Understand how production issues are diagnosed.
- Learn why the existing system works the way it does.
- Understand the decisions behind the architecture.

The useful question becomes:

> **What would make me better at my current job in 6 months?**

rather than:

> What trending technology should I learn next?

**Takeaway:** Depth in your existing domain can be more valuable than constantly adding another technology to your résumé.

---

## 3. Understand the code AI writes

Several developers suggested using the additional time to understand AI-generated code rather than blindly accepting it.

The reasoning is simple:

AI can make implementation faster, but that doesn't automatically mean the developer understands:

- Why the code works.
- Why it was structured that way.
- What assumptions it makes.
- What edge cases exist.
- Whether the implementation is actually appropriate.
- What could break in production.

One commenter specifically suggested going back through code submitted with AI assistance and asking yourself whether you actually understand it.

**Takeaway:** AI should increase your implementation speed without decreasing your understanding.

---

## 4. Use AI to increase your leverage, not just your typing speed

The discussion suggests a broader way of thinking about AI.

If AI allows you to finish your normal work in half the time, the goal shouldn't necessarily be:

**"Give me twice as many tickets."**

The additional capacity can instead be used for:

- Better architecture.
- Automation.
- Developer tooling.
- Documentation.
- Code quality.
- Technical debt.
- Research.
- Side projects.
- Helping teammates.
- Learning deeper concepts.

The opportunity is to move **up the abstraction ladder**.

---

## 5. Find the problems nobody wants to touch

One of the strongest practical suggestions was:

> Find problems in the codebase nobody else wants to touch.

Examples included:

- Technical debt.
- Flaky tests.
- Legacy modules.
- Poor documentation.
- Old bugs.
- Unpleasant maintenance work.
- Areas everyone avoids because they're difficult to understand.

These problems often have an interesting property:

**They may not be assigned to anyone, but solving them creates visible value.**

And unlike repeatedly solving interview problems, fixing real problems in your company's codebase can build:

- Domain knowledge.
- System understanding.
- Reputation.
- Trust.
- Technical judgment.

**Takeaway:** Look for valuable problems that fall between the cracks.

---

## 6. Build real things

Another recurring recommendation was to use free capacity to build things.

They don't necessarily need to become startups.

They can be:

- Small tools.
- Automation.
- AI experiments.
- Developer utilities.
- Personal projects.
- Open-source projects.
- Something you've always wanted to build.

The important part is **actually building**, rather than endlessly consuming tutorials.

One commenter specifically suggested building real side projects and learning how to build your own AI harness.

---

## 7. Explore AI deeply while the technology is changing

One commenter argued that the current AI productivity gains may eventually change engineering expectations.

Their suggestion was to use the current period to learn AI deeply:

- Build with AI.
- Understand AI tooling.
- Experiment with AI workflows.
- Build your own tools.
- Explore areas that your current job doesn't require yet.

Another commenter suggested learning how LLMs/AI/ML work internally and studying the mathematics behind them.

**Takeaway:** If AI is increasing your productivity today, understanding the technology behind that productivity can become a career advantage.

---

## 8. Learn fundamentals instead of another framework

The thread contains a useful counterpoint to technology chasing:

Rather than learning yet another framework, choose something **boring but valuable** and go deep.

Examples mentioned include:

- System design.
- Debugging production issues.
- Better documentation.
- Architecture.
- Core computer science.
- Understanding existing systems.

These skills tend to remain valuable even when specific technologies change.

**Takeaway:** Don't confuse learning more technologies with becoming a better engineer.

---

## 9. Use free time to prepare for the next career move

Some commenters interpreted substantial free time as a reason to prepare for a possible job change.

Suggestions included:

- Interview preparation.
- DSA.
- System design.
- Learning new areas.
- Building projects.
- Open source.
- Improving your résumé.
- Developing a backup plan.

There was disagreement in the thread about whether significant free time necessarily indicates job insecurity, so that interpretation shouldn't be treated as a fact.

The useful part is simply:

**If your current workload gives you unexpected capacity, you can invest some of that capacity in making yourself more resilient.**

---

## 10. Open source is a good use of spare capacity

One suggestion was to contribute to open source.

Potential benefits:

- Learning unfamiliar codebases.
- Working with developers outside your company.
- Practicing collaboration.
- Building a public track record.
- Learning new technologies.
- Having something tangible to discuss during interviews.

---

## 11. Learn outside technology too

Not every response suggested more coding.

Some people recommended using the time to:

- Read books.
- Learn another language.
- Study mathematics.
- Exercise.
- Learn about nutrition and health.
- Explore hobbies.
- Read about unrelated subjects.
- Build social connections.

One commenter explicitly challenged the idea that work should consume every available minute:

> Your free time is dream of many.

**Takeaway:** Productivity isn't the only worthwhile use of time.

---

## 12. Build optionality

A few responses suggested using the extra time to develop additional sources of income or alternative career paths.

Ideas included:

- Freelancing.
- Side projects.
- Open-source projects.
- Content creation.
- Building products.
- Learning about investments.
- Developing skills outside your current role.

The underlying idea is:

**Don't let your entire future depend on one employer, one technology or one skill.**

---

## 13. Don't turn learning into another treadmill

The original poster already noticed something important:

> Constantly learning new/trending tech is exhausting.

That's worth paying attention to.

There is a difference between:

**Learning because something is valuable**

and

**Learning because you feel guilty whenever you're not learning.**

The second can become an endless treadmill:

New framework → new AI tool → new language → new framework → new certification → new trend...

Eventually you know many things superficially without developing deep capability.

**Takeaway:** Choose learning based on your direction, not on the existence of a new trend.

# A useful framework

If AI gives you 2–3 extra hours of capacity every day, consider dividing that capacity across four areas:

### 1. Current job

Become significantly better at what you already do.

- Architecture
- Debugging
- Production
- Codebase
- Domain knowledge
- Developer tooling

### 2. Career capital

Build skills that increase your future options.

- DSA
- System design
- CS fundamentals
- Communication
- Leadership
- Another technology stack

### 3. Exploration

Experiment with things that might become important.

- AI
- New tools
- Research
- Side projects
- Open source

### 4. Life

Don't forget:

- Exercise
- Reading
- Family
- Friends
- Hobbies
- Rest

The objective isn't to maximize the number of productive hours.

It's to **make better use of the capacity AI has created.**

---

## What I want to remember

AI changing how quickly I complete tasks should change **what I do with my time**, not just how many tasks I complete.

If I can finish my assigned work much faster:

**Don't automatically ask for more tickets.**

Ask:

> **What higher-value thing can I do with the capacity I've created?**

That could mean understanding the system more deeply, solving neglected problems, building tools, learning AI, developing career capital, helping teammates, or simply having some genuine downtime.

### The progression

**AI makes me faster → I gain capacity → I invest that capacity deliberately → my capabilities and leverage increase.**

That's a much more useful way to think about AI productivity than simply:

**AI makes me faster → company gives me more work.**
